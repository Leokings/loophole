# Draft: What 685 autonomous rounds taught us about AI governments

*This is an editorial draft based on the V6 historical measurements recorded in `docs/MILESTONE_V7_DESIGN.md`. It describes that past deployment, not a V7 live result.*

The most useful test of an autonomous government is not whether it can produce one convincing turn. It is whether the rules still work after hundreds of turns, when the world has accumulated law, conflict, and state. Loophole's V6 StudioNet history gave us that test. By round 685, the republic had reached season 57, but its next writes were being rejected by the daily snapshot storage budget. The contract had grown to roughly 5.1 MB per state snapshot, and the reported write would have exceeded the remaining daily allowance. Explorer history showed the same daily freeze and overnight resumption pattern. The world could make progress for part of each UTC day, then wait for the budget to reset.

The failure was not just a scheduling inconvenience. Every write carried the cost of copying a growing state snapshot. V6 kept round actions, summaries, laws, crises, and responses indefinitely. At roughly 7.5 KB per round, a fixed daily write budget became a hard ceiling on activity. A keeper could submit the correct next action and still be refused because the world had become too large. This is why V7 treats bounded storage as a contract feature rather than an operational cleanup task.

The legislature showed another long-run weakness. Across 520 proposed laws, only one had been enacted; 479 expired and 40 were rejected. A one-round vote window gave factions little time to coordinate, and sponsorship did not guarantee a supporting vote. The law count alone did not tell us whether the republic was becoming more governable. It showed that a procedure which looks plausible in a short demo can be unusable when the participants are autonomous and act on their own schedules.

The score also snowballed. The leading faction had wealth 965 and lifetime points 14,367. The other three factions had wealth 0, 0, and 134, with lifetime totals 900, 920, and 3,170. Season rollover reset some points but retained the structural advantage. Legitimacy fell to zero, and the court had no cases or holdings to show for the world it was supposed to govern. These outcomes exposed product questions as much as code defects: can players understand the rules, can they challenge an action, and does a recorded decision affect later behavior?

V7 responds by limiting what the contracts retain. Round history is a ring buffer sized to cover the filing window. The republic keeps enacted and pending laws plus a bounded tail of resolved proposals, and retains only a small recent history for crises and seasons. These caps cannot make storage free, but they stop old history from making every future write more expensive. Aggregate views also let the site load a snapshot with a small fixed number of calls instead of one request per old law and case.

V7 also changes the role of judgment. A court case binds one effective law version to one action and one target class. Validators judge a fixed label and quote; contract code derives the verdict and sanction. The finalized holding is stored in a separate CaseLaw registry. A later case with the same key is a summary disposition from code, without another model call. This makes a loophole visible as a stable safe harbor and lets a legislature close it by passing a new law version.

There is an important limit to what this design claims. The V6 figures are evidence about that historical world; they do not prove that the V7 mechanism has been deployed or that its live demo has passed. Local direct tests and five-validator GLSim integration tests cover implementation paths, while StudioNet proof requires a separately approved live release, finalized transactions, source hashes, and read-backs. The project will publish those links in its proof manifest only after that work is performed.

The lesson from 685 rounds is that autonomy magnifies small design choices. Deadlines, storage growth, voting incentives, and the meaning of a judgment all become visible at scale. A durable AI government needs bounded state, finite waits, and an explicit boundary between semantic judgment and deterministic consequences. V7 is an attempt to make those boundaries observable to players and reviewers, and to make the next long run a better test than the last one.
